\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Magnetic fields are fundamental to the physics of molecular clouds, influencing their formation, structure, and evolution toward star formation \citep{mckee2007,crutcher2012magnetic,hennebelle2019role,pattle2023}. In low-density cloud envelopes, magnetic support can oppose gravitational contraction \citep{mouschovias1976nonhomologous}, while in collapsing cores, magnetic braking transfers angular momentum and affects the subsequent evolution of the gas \citep{pudritz2019}. The interplay between magnetic fields, turbulence, and gravity influences star formation efficiency \citep{krumholz2019,federrath2015inefficient} and shapes the filamentary structures in which stars form \citep{li2014link}. Understanding these processes requires observational constraints on both magnetic field strength and geometry. Measurements of the plane-of-sky and line-of-sight components provide complementary information about the field and its spatial variations across molecular clouds.

The plane-of-sky component, $B_\perp$, is commonly studied through polarized thermal emission from magnetically aligned dust grains and polarization of background starlight \citep{heiles20009286,andersson2015interstellar}. These observations trace the projected field orientation and allow its relationship with the cloud's gas and dust structures to be investigated. Dust polarization observations, including those from \textit{Planck}, have provided extensive maps of projected magnetic field orientations in molecular clouds \citep{planck2016,soler2019using}. They therefore offer important constraints on how the field is organized relative to filaments and other cloud structures.

Measuring the line-of-sight magnetic field component, $B_\parallel$, remains observationally challenging. Zeeman splitting provides direct estimates of this component, but weak signals and the need for suitable spectral lines limit the number of available measurements \citep{crutcher1999detection,crutcher2010magnetic,crutcher2019review}. Faraday rotation toward polarized background radio sources offers a complementary means of investigating $B_\parallel$ across molecular clouds \citep{wolleben2004,tahani2018}. Combined with estimates of the electron column density and the rotation contributed by material outside the cloud, these measurements constrain the line-of-sight field strength and its direction toward or away from the observer.

Faraday rotation is the rotation of the plane of linear polarization as radiation propagates through a magnetized medium containing free electrons. For a single Faraday-thin component behind an external rotating screen, the polarization angle follows
$\Psi(\lambda)=\Psi_0+\mathrm{RM}\lambda^2$, where $\Psi_0$ is the intrinsic polarization angle, $\lambda$ is the wavelength, and $\mathrm{RM}$ is the rotation measure \citep{burn1966,brentjens2005}. Under these conditions, RM equals the Faraday depth,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rm-basic}
\Phi = 0.812 \int_{\rm source}^{\rm observer} n_e B_\parallel,dl
\quad [\mathrm{rad,m^{-2}}],
\end{equation}
where $n_e$ is the free electron density in cm$^{-3}$, $B_\parallel$ is in $\mu$G, and $dl$ is in pc. Positive $B_\parallel$ denotes a field directed toward the observer \citep{han2017observing}. The measured rotation depends on both the magnetic field and the electron density along the propagation path. More complex configurations of emitting and rotating material need not follow a single linear relation between polarization angle and $\lambda^2$.

Two steps are needed to estimate a molecular cloud's $B_\parallel$ from the observed RMs. First, the rotation contributed by material outside the cloud must be estimated and subtracted. The observed RM can be written schematically as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rm-split}
\mathrm{RM_{obs}} =
\mathrm{RM_{fg}}+\mathrm{RM_{cloud}}+\mathrm{RM_{bg}},
\end{equation}
where the foreground and background terms represent contributions outside the target cloud, including any contribution associated with the background source. Second, an electron column density must be estimated to convert $\mathrm{RM_{cloud}}$ into a field strength. The resulting field estimate represents an electron-density-weighted average through the cloud toward each background source. Its reliability depends on both the subtraction of the non-cloud RM and the adopted electron column density.

\citet{tahani2018} developed a method that combines background-source RMs, cloud extinction maps, and chemical modeling to carry out these steps. Background sources projected onto nearby low-extinction regions are used as reference points to estimate the RM contribution outside the cloud. The extinction map and modeled electron abundances then provide estimates of the electron column density through the cloud. This method is implemented in the publicly available \texttt{MC-BLOS} (Molecular Cloud Line-of-Sight Magnetic Field) software \citep{tahani2025mcblos}. The software provides a systematic procedure for selecting reference points, calculating the cloud RM contribution, and deriving $B_\parallel$ and its uncertainties. Its treatment of electron abundance as a function of cloud depth allows changes in ionization conditions to be included when estimating the field strength.

The Perseus Molecular Cloud is a nearby star-forming region where improved sampling can extend earlier measurements of $B_\parallel$. At a distance of $294\pm17$~pc \citep{zucker2018}, it has extensive observations of extinction, dust emission, and molecular gas \citep{ridge2006complete,kainulainen2009,lombardi2009nicest,andre2010,curtis2010submillimetre}. Using the NVSS-based RM catalog of \citet{taylor2009}, \citet{tahani2018} obtained line-of-sight field estimates toward Perseus and reported an indication of a field reversal across the cloud. However, these estimates were available at only approximately ten positions through the cloud. More measurements are needed to examine the distribution of field strengths and determine how consistently the inferred directions follow this earlier pattern.


Previous observations have constrained the magnetic field in Perseus
across several spatial scales. OH Zeeman measurements toward Barnard~1
yield $B_\parallel=+27\pm4~\mu\mathrm{G}$
\citep{goodman1989measurement}, while observations with a larger beam give
$+19.1\pm3.9~\mu\mathrm{G}$ \citep{crutcher1993oh}.
Zeeman detections toward L1448-CO and L1448-COe give
$+26.0\pm3.7$ and $+20.6\pm3.4~\mu\mathrm{G}$, respectively
\citep{troland2008magnetic}. These values are expressed using our convention
that positive $B_\parallel$ points toward the observer, reversing
the signs reported in the original Zeeman studies.
On larger scales, the Faraday-rotation measurements of
\citet{tahani2018} yield error-weighted mean fields of approximately
$+136$ and $-90~\mu\mathrm{G}$ for sight lines directed toward and
away from the observer, respectively \citep{tahani2022b}.
Complementary dust-polarization analyses estimate
$B_\perp=120\pm60~\mu\mathrm{G}$ in Barnard~1
\citep{coude2019jcmt}. In IC~348, Davis--Chandrasekhar--Fermi estimates
give $B_\perp=102\pm52~\mu\mathrm{G}$ in the filament and
$394\pm170$ and $417\pm182~\mu\mathrm{G}$ in the HH~211~MMS
and IC~348~MMS cores, respectively, with lower strengths obtained
using an alternative estimator for compressible turbulence
\citep{choi2024jcmt}. At protostellar scales, an ALMA analysis
of the SVS~13A streamer estimates
$B_\perp=1.1\pm0.6~\mathrm{mG}$ \citep{cortes2025first}.
These results probe different field components, gas conditions,
and spatial scales, and therefore do not define a single
representative field strength for Perseus. They motivate improved
spatial sampling $B_\parallel$ across the cloud.

In Paper~I \citep{hajizadeh2026comprehensive}, we presented a dedicated VLA L-band polarization survey of Perseus covering approximately $13.8$~deg$^2$. The survey provides wideband RM measurements for 205 high-confidence polarized sources, corresponding to approximately $14.8$ sources per square degree. This catalog substantially increases the number of sampled positions and provides the observational basis for the present study.

Our aim is to use these measurements to determine the strength and direction of the line-of-sight magnetic field at individual positions across Perseus. We apply \texttt{MC-BLOS} to the VLA RM catalog, together with an extinction map and chemical-model electron abundances, to obtain $B_\parallel$ estimates at 197 positions. We examine the distribution of field strengths, the locations of fields directed toward and away from the observer, and the relation between the inferred field strengths and extinction. We also assess the uncertainties and the sensitivity of the results to reference-point selection, since a change in the reference RM can affect both the magnitude and sign of the inferred field.

The main advance of this work is the increase from approximately ten positions with cloud field estimates in the earlier analysis to 197 positions within our survey footprint. This denser sampling allows us to examine spatial variations in $B_\parallel$ in regions that were previously poorly sampled and reassess the suggested field reversal with a larger set of measurements. The resulting catalog provides an expanded observational basis for studying the line-of-sight magnetic field in Perseus.

The remainder of this paper presents the observational data, describes the application of \texttt{MC-BLOS}, and reports the derived $B_\parallel$ estimates and their spatial distribution. We then discuss the comparison with previous measurements, the sensitivity of the results to the analysis assumptions, and the limitations of the inferred field strengths before summarizing our conclusions.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Rotation Measure Catalog}

The RM catalog used here is drawn from the wideband VLA L-band polarization
survey presented in Paper~I \citep{hajizadeh2026comprehensive}. It uses the
2019 VLA D-configuration observations (program 19B-053), covering
$\sim 13.8$~deg$^2$ in four mosaic regions. The original setup covered 930--2070~MHz in 18 spectral
windows; nine 64~MHz windows were retained after RFI flagging, with central
frequencies spanning approximately 1346--2038~MHz. Polarization spectra were
formed from 72 frequency bins of width 8~MHz, smoothed to a common
$60\arcsec$ beam. 

The mean Stokes~$I$ sensitivity is $\sim 80$~$\mu$Jy~beam$^{-1}$, yielding a
catalog of 1410 compact radio sources. RMs were derived using RM Synthesis
\citep{burn1966,brentjens2005} and RM CLEAN \citep{heald2009}, with a
conservative $8\sigma$ threshold on the Faraday-spectrum peak. Paper~I
reports 205 polarized sources, all of which were used here, with a source density of $\sim 14.8$~deg$^{-2}$. This is more than
ten times the $\sim 1.25$~deg$^{-2}$ density of the NVSS-based RM catalog
in the same region reported in Paper~I. 


\subsection{Extinction Map}


  \texttt{MC-BLOS} requires an extinction map to identify potential OFF-cloud
  reference points and to estimate the electron column density toward
  ON-cloud sources through chemical modeling. We use the 2MASS-based
  near-infrared extinction map of Perseus described by
  \citet{kainulainen2009}, constructed using the NICEST color-excess
  method \citep{lombardi2009nicest}. This method estimates dust extinction
  from the near-infrared colors of background stars. The visual-extinction
  ($A_V$) map used here has a pixel spacing of $1.5$~arcmin, distinct
  from its effective angular resolution. All 205 RM sources in our input
  catalog are represented in the RM--extinction matched sample.

  Following \citet{tahani2025mcblos}, we relate visual extinction to total
  hydrogen column density using
  \begin{equation}
  N_{\rm H}
  = 2.21 \times 10^{21}
  \left(\frac{A_V}{\mathrm{mag}}\right)\mathrm{cm}^{-2},
  \end{equation}
  where $N_{\rm H}=N(\mathrm{H\,I})+2N(\mathrm{H}_2)$
  \citep{guver2009relation}. For each ON-cloud point, we estimate
  the cloud-associated extinction by subtracting the mean extinction
  of the selected OFF references,
  \begin{equation}
  A_{V,\rm cloud}=A_{V,\rm ON}-A_{V,\rm ref}.
  \end{equation}
  This corrected extinction determines the integration depth through
  the chemical model used to estimate the electron column density.

\section{Methodology}
  \label{sec:method}
We used \texttt{MC-BLOS} v1.0\footnote{\url{https://github.com/MehrnooshTahani/MC-BLOS}} \citep{tahani2025mcblos} to derive $B_\parallel$ from the VLA RM catalog and extinction map described above. In the following subsections, we describe the reference-point selection, electron column density estimates, adopted parameters, and uncertainty calculations used for Perseus. Full descriptions of the underlying method and software are provided by \citet{tahani2018} and \citet{tahani2025mcblos}.


  \subsection{Reference Point Selection}

  \texttt{MC-BLOS} separates the cloud's contribution to the observed RM from the Galactic foreground and background using an ON--OFF approach \citep{tahani2018}. Sources with extinction below a threshold $A_{V,\rm th}$ are treated as potential OFF points. For Perseus, at $b\simeq-20^\circ$, we adopt the software's default of $A_{V,\rm th}=1.0$~mag for clouds with $|b|>15^\circ$. These clouds are relatively isolated from the complex Galactic plane, allowing a lower extinction threshold to identify potential reference points \citep{tahani2025mcblos}.

  The software then rejects potential OFF points that lie too close to
  high-extinction regions, where the cloud's own field could contribute, and
  those whose RM is a statistical outlier relative to the rest of the OFF
  sample. The proximity and outlier criteria are controlled by the multipliers listed
in Table~\ref{tab:params}. For Perseus, these reject sources within
a square extending 20 pixels (30~arcmin) in each map coordinate around
regions with $A_V > A_{V,th}\times 5$~mag. This is a box-based proximity test, not a circular
angular-distance cut. The algorithm also rejects
RM values more than 5 interquartile ranges from the initial candidate-sample median.

  The number of reference points used is set by the stability trend
  analysis of \citet{tahani2018}, automated in v1.0. $B_\parallel$ is computed
  for every ON data point as OFF points are added in order of increasing
  extinction, and the number at which the resulting values stabilize is adopted.
  We present the outcome of this analysis for our catalog in
  Section~\ref{sec:rmref}.

  \subsection{Cloud RM and Field Direction}

  For each ON point the cloud's contribution is the difference between the
  observed RM and the reference value,
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rmcloud}
      \mathrm{RM_{cloud}} = \mathrm{RM_{obs}} - \mathrm{RM_{ref}},
  \end{equation}
  where $\mathrm{RM_{ref}}$ is the average RM of the selected reference points.
  A positive $\mathrm{RM_{cloud}}$ corresponds to a field directed toward the
  observer and a negative value to a field directed away. Because the same
  scalar $\mathrm{RM_{ref}}$ is subtracted from every point, the resulting
  sign pattern depends on this single zero point, and we test its robustness in
  Section~\ref{sec:rmref-sensitivity}.

  \subsection{Electron Column Density and Field Strength}
  \label{sec:ne}

  Field strength requires the electron column density along each line-of-sight.
  Rather than assuming a fixed ionization fraction, MC-BLOS reads the electron
  abundance from a chemical evolution code \citep{gibson2009} using the UMIST
  Rate99 reaction network \citep{leteuff2000}. The cloud is treated as a
  symmetric plane-parallel slab: the abundance is summed layer by layer from the
  edge inward until half the total extinction is reached, and the result is
  doubled to account for the far side \citep[see Figure~1
  of][]{tahani2025mcblos}. This captures the change in ionization conditions
  between diffuse envelopes and dense interiors.

  Writing the electron column density as $N_e = \int n_e \, dl$ in
  cm$^{-3}$~pc, consistent with Equation~\ref{eq:rm-basic}, the field strength
  follows from
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:blos}
      B_\parallel \;[\mu\mathrm{G}] =
      \frac{\mathrm{RM_{cloud}} \;[\mathrm{rad\,m^{-2}}]}
           {0.812 \, N_e \;[\mathrm{cm^{-3}\,pc}]}.
  \end{equation}

  \subsection{Input Parameters}

  Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the parameters we adopted for Perseus. We ran the
  software with its default configuration except where noted, having first
  inspected the diagnostic outputs as recommended by \citet{tahani2025mcblos}. The chemical
  model assumes $n_0 = 1000$~cm$^{-3}$, $T_0 = 12$~K, and a Habing field of
  $G_0 = 1$. Reference point selection uses the stability trend option with a
  minimum of five reference points. No quadrant weighting was applied; the
  reference RM is a simple (unweighted) average of all selected OFF points. The
  ON-point extinction multiplier was set to its default value of 1.0.
  The archived pipeline configuration uses a distance of 250~pc and a Jeans
  length of 1~pc to set the proximity box. Physical sampling scales quoted
  here use 294~pc. The ON selection requires
  $A_V\geq m\langle A_V\rangle_{\rm ref}$ after excluding selected OFF points;
  $m=1$ gives a cutoff of 0.553~mag. We also examine $m=3$ below.

  \subsection{Uncertainties}

  We estimate the uncertainties in $B_\parallel$ following
  \citet{tahani2025mcblos}, accounting for uncertainties in RM and
  extinction and for the sensitivity of the chemical model to temperature
  and density. The RM contribution includes the measurement uncertainty
  of each source and the standard error of the mean RM of the selected
  OFF-cloud references. Following the software prescription, these two
  RM uncertainties are added linearly before propagation to
  $B_\parallel$.

  The extinction contribution is estimated using the minimum and maximum
  valid extinction values within a $5\times5$-pixel region centred on each
  source's matched extinction pixel. The region extends two pixels in
  each coordinate, with its size determined from the largest positional
  uncertainty in the input RM catalog and the map pixel spacing of
  $1.5$~arcmin. The same region size is used for all sources. This
  estimates sensitivity to local extinction variations rather than the
  full systematic uncertainty of the extinction map. Chemical-model
  sensitivity is evaluated by varying the temperature by $\pm20\%$
  ($9.6$--$14.4$~K) and the density by $\pm50\%$
  ($500$--$1500$~cm$^{-3}$).

  The resulting RM, extinction, temperature, and density contributions
  are combined in quadrature to obtain upper and lower uncertainties.
  The software additionally applies a clipping prescription intended to
  prevent the non-RM contributions alone from implying a field reversal.
  These asymmetric uncertainties are model-dependent estimates, not
  formal Gaussian confidence intervals.

  For a positive electron column density, the field direction is set by
  the sign of $\mathrm{RM_{obs}}-\mathrm{RM_{ref}}$. Extinction and
  chemical-model variations therefore affect the inferred field strength,
  whereas RM uncertainties can make its direction ambiguous. We assess
  the significance of the field directions separately in
  Section~\ref{sec:signs}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Reference RM Determination}
\label{sec:rmref}

All 205 polarized sources from Paper~I are matched to the extinction
  map. Of these, 18 satisfy the initial OFF-reference criterion,
  $A_V \leq 1.0$~mag. Four candidates (IDs 18, 27, 132, and 170) are
  rejected because their surrounding proximity boxes contain pixels
  with $A_V > 5.0$~mag, leaving 14 candidates. The anomalous-RM filter
  rejects no sources, and the far-cloud exclusion is disabled.
  We additionally impose a minimum separation of 1.2~arcmin between
  OFF candidates. This excludes ID 197 in favour of the nearby ID 198,
  which has the same extinction but a smaller RM uncertainty.
  The stability-based selection adopts eight of the 13 eligible
  candidates as OFF references; the remaining five are eligible but
  not selected. Figure~\ref{fig:off-selection} shows the initial
  candidate pool, and Table~\ref{tab:off-audit} lists the selection
  outcome for each source. Sources not selected as OFF references
  remain available for the ON analysis, subject to the ON extinction
  criterion.




\input{off_candidate_audit.tex}





To determine how many OFF references to adopt, MC-BLOS examines the
  stability of the inferred magnetic fields as reference candidates
  are added in order of increasing extinction. At each step, the
  reference RM and mean reference extinction are recalculated, and
  $B_\parallel$ is recomputed for each OFF point. The aim is to
  identify a reference count beyond which adding further candidates
  produces relatively small changes in the inferred fields.

  The stability algorithm compares successive $B_\parallel$ values
  for each point and identifies the beginning of its longest
  sequence of changes below a trial tolerance. It finds the most
  frequent starting count across sight lines and repeats this
  procedure over a range of tolerances. The most frequently favoured
  count satisfying the selection constraints, including a minimum
  of five references, is adopted. This procedure selects eight
  OFF references for Perseus.

  Figure~\ref{fig:stability} summarizes these trends. The solid blue
  line represents the median $B_\parallel$ across sight lines, while
  the darker and lighter shaded regions show the 25th--75th and
  10th--90th percentile ranges, respectively. These bands represent
  the spread of field values among RM points, not confidence
  intervals. The median changes substantially when only a few
  references are used, whereas changes beyond approximately eight
  references are smaller, although fluctuations remain. The dashed
  vertical line marks the adopted count, $N=8$. This summary
  illustrates the reference-count dependence; the selection itself
  is based on the individual RM point trends.

  The eight selected references have RMs spanning
  6.0--58.6~rad~m$^{-2}$ and a mean extinction of
  $\langle A_V\rangle_{\rm ref}=0.553$~mag. Their unweighted mean
  defines the reference RM:
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rmref}
  \mathrm{RM_{ref}} = 31.6 \pm 6.1~\mathrm{rad\,m^{-2}},
  \end{equation}
  where the quoted uncertainty is the standard error of the mean.
  The sample standard deviation is 17.2~rad~m$^{-2}$.

For comparison, we sampled the version-2 Galactic Faraday rotation map of \citet{hutschenreuter2022galactic} at $(l,b)=(159^\circ,-20^\circ)$, a representative position toward Perseus. The corresponding HEALPix pixel gives $41.8 \pm 16.8$~rad~m$^{-2}$, where the uncertainty is the posterior standard deviation supplied with the map. This estimate is approximately $10$~rad~m$^{-2}$ higher than our adopted reference RM of $31.6 \pm 6.1$~rad~m$^{-2}$, although the difference is smaller than the map uncertainty. The two estimates represent different quantities: the reconstruction describes the total Galactic Faraday rotation toward this position and may include a contribution from Perseus, whereas our reference RM is derived from selected OFF-cloud points to estimate the contribution from material outside the cloud. We therefore use the map value as contextual information and retain the OFF-cloud reference for the magnetic field calculation.



\subsection{Classification of RM points}
\label{sec:classification}

After removing the 8 adopted reference points, the remaining 197 sources are
treated as ON-cloud sight lines. Five candidates remain eligible after separation but are not selected;
a sixth (ID 197) is excluded by the separation rule. All six
they remain in the ON sample because the stability procedure selects the
reference zero point rather than defining an additional exclusion criterion.
Their extinctions span $A_V = 0.65$--$15.6$~mag with a mean of 2.43~mag.

% can add further dependence. Consequently the quoted binomial probability
% is not a calibrated significance for a cloud-scale reversal and does not establish
% spatial clustering. We therefore treat the north--west versus south--east
% arrangement as a spatial pattern visible in the map and do not assign it a
% separate clustering significance without a prespecified spatial statistic.


\subsubsection{Exhaustive Eight-Point Reference Combinations}
\label{sec:all-reference-combinations}
\label{sec:rmref-sensitivity}

We recalculated the magnetic-field map for all $\binom{14}{8}=3003$
combinations of eight filtered OFF candidates and compared the maps in an
animation. This exploratory ensemble includes combinations that violate the
1.2~arcmin pair-separation rule of the adopted selection; it tests a broader
reference pool rather than only admissible pipeline selections. The resulting reference RMs span 22.2--43.9~rad~m$^{-2}$.
Although field strengths and some individual signs change, several groups
retain their direction throughout this range: positive fields persist in
the western part of the map and around RA $\simeq50$--$51^\circ$,
Dec $\simeq31^\circ$, while a group of negative fields persists in the
southern cloud.

Among the 189 RM points present in every combination, 77 remain positive
and 35 remain negative throughout; the other 77 change sign. Thus, the
oppositely directed fields in these specific parts of the cloud persist
despite changes in the reference selection. Their persistence supports
the spatial variation in field direction, although the precise boundary
between positive and negative regions remains sensitive to the OFF-point
choice.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity to the ON-Point Extinction Cut}
\label{sec:on-cut}

Keeping the same eight references and chemical model, increasing the
ON-point multiplier from $m=1$ to $m=3$ raises the observed-extinction cutoff
from 0.553 to 1.659~mag. The retained sample decreases from 197 to 134
sight lines, with 87 positive and 47 negative fields (65\% and 35\%).
The nominal field value and sign of every retained sight line are identical
in the two runs: only sample membership changes. The similar positive
fraction shows that the global sign imbalance is not confined to the
lowest-extinction sight lines; it is not an independent test of the
reference RM or of the spatial reversal. We retain $m=1$ for the main
catalog and use $m=3$ only as this selection check. Comparisons here use
nominal fields, not the multiplier-3 uncertainty estimates, whose
sensitivity tables require alignment by source ID before propagation.
